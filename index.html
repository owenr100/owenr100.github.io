E-bike research

Which road characteristics, like speed limit and number of lanes, are most closely tied to e-bike crashes in California? This page walks through the research, from the data to the recommendations.

Introduction

E-bikes are an attractive way for cities to promote green transportation. But as e-bikes have grown in popularity over the past 5–10 years, injury and fatality rates have gone up. A study by the University of California, San Francisco found that from 2017 to 2022, hospitalizations from e-bike accidents increased 99% a year.

My research aims to identify the road characteristics most closely tied to e-bike accidents, giving city planners information they can use to keep roads safe for bikers and e-bikers. There is very little existing data on where e-bike crashes happen and why.

Research question: How do road properties influence the risk of e-bike crashes in California and Sonoma County?

Methodology

  1. Crash data. I used the California Crash Reporting System (CCRS), which includes all motor vehicle crashes reported to the California Highway Patrol between 2016 and 2026, and pulled out the e-bike crashes.
  2. Road data. I combined the crashes with OpenStreetMap, which has information about the roads where they happened, such as speed limits and lanes.
  3. Analysis. I graphed how road characteristics relate to e-bike crashes, statewide and in Sonoma County.
  4. Mapping. I plotted the crashes from 2021 to 2025 on an interactive map.

Key findings

E-bike crashes in the CCRS data were rare before 2020, then climbed every year after.

  • Faster roads see more crashes. Statewide, 1,586 crashes were reported on roads with a speed limit of 30 mph or more, compared with 1,046 on roads of 25 mph or less. Since there are far more miles of 25 mph road, crashes happen disproportionately on faster roads.
  • Bike lanes help. Only about 15% of crashes happened on a road with a dedicated bike lane. That’s low, since many e-bikers ride more than 15% of their miles on roads with a bike lane.
  • Rider behavior matters. When fault was logged, the e-bike rider was at fault in 82% of crashes.

Recommendations

  • Encourage riding on slower roads. This reduces injury severity and how often e-bikes interact with cars.
  • Add bike lanes where slower roads aren’t an option. When residential roads can’t be used, such as at a freeway or waterway crossing, a dedicated bike lane is a good alternative.
  • Require traffic education and risk warnings. With e-bikers at fault in 82% of crashes where fault was logged, training riders would likely prevent many accidents.

Future directions

This research has limits that future work could address:

  • Only reported crashes. CCRS only includes crashes reported to the California Highway Patrol, so many minor e-bike crashes are missing.
  • Counts, not rates. There’s no data on how many miles people ride on each kind of road, so a road type with more crashes might just have more riders. Tracking real rides would make it possible to compare crashes per mile.
  • Gaps in CCRS data. Not every collision entry had a log of the speed limit or number of lanes.
  • More road features. Future work could look at other characteristics, like lane width and road quality.

Related research & resources

Related project: mileage estimates

In a separate project, I looked at how accurately people estimate how much they ride and drive. People who received a subsidized e-bike used an app that tracked their mileage and mode of transportation, then filled out a survey estimating how far they had traveled. Each graph shows the gap between a person’s survey estimate and the mileage the app recorded (“Value”): above 0 means they overestimated, below 0 means they underestimated.

Select a figure to enlarge it.

So what?

After getting an e-bike, people overestimated how much they biked and underestimated how much they drove. Many evaluations of e-bike programs, including subsidy programs, rely on surveys like this one, so they may make e-bikes look like they replace more car trips than they really do. Tracking trips with an app gives a more accurate picture.

It matters for crash research too. To compare how risky different roads are, we need to know how many miles people actually ride on them, and self-reported mileage would get that wrong.
